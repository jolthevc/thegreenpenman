# Editorial standard | The Green Pen Man

## One-sentence directive parables

Write one **grammatically complete sentence** of advice, warning, principle, or instruction. Usually 8–20 words; occasionally longer if justified. The line should be immediately understandable, memorable, and meaningfully applicable to life. It can use imagery, a short tale, surprise, juxtaposition, and irony.

**North star:** The reader understands the words instantly, sees the drawing, then understands something deeper about their own life.

### Strong constructions (mix naturally)
- **Never [seek advice from a conflicted source]**: "Never ask the man selling umbrellas whether a storm is coming."
- **Do not [confuse one thing with another]**: "Do not confuse the silence of fear with the peace of respect."
- **[Action] before [consequence]**: "Choose the sky before the cage begins to look like home."
- **[Action] what [was not yours]**: "Put down what was never yours to carry."
- **[Warning] about the hidden trade-off**: "Do not thank the fence until you know which side of it you may need."
These examples were favorites during direction-setting, not all publication-approved copy.

### Requirements
1. Give genuinely **prescriptive** advice or a clear decision principle. A reflective insight can occasionally work, but it should teach something actionable.
2. **One sentence** only. Avoid the earlier formula "The cage was open. The bird had forgotten the sky."
3. Be specific and imageable. Prefer "umbrella seller / storm" to "watch for conflicts of interest".
4. Make the advice *actually defensible*. "Never trust anyone" is false and unhelpful. Figurative imperatives can be forceful without promoting paranoia.
5. Wording must be lean, elegant and readable on a phone. No four-clause labyrinths.
6. Build memorable contrast, hidden motive, ironic cost, or consequence. The metaphor should earn its place.
7. Do not over-index on betrayal, wolves, cages, trauma or gloomy advice. Include courage, patience, friendship, perspective, work, contentment, integrity, gratitude and purposeful risk.
8. Avoid familiar internet clichés rephrased with prettier nouns. Ask whether the reader actually learns something.
9. Avoid uncredited modern quotations; known ancient proverbs should be identified where relevant. Verify distinctive attributed phrases before calling them original.
10. No em dashes.

### Important writing distinctions
- **Too abstract**: "Some prisons are built from shelter." No concrete action.
- **Too explanatory**: "The bird stayed because learned helplessness made it fearful of taking a risk." A psychology lecture.
- **Too sentimental**: "Even the smallest kindness can change the whole world." Unearned generalization.
- **Too ornate**: "Depart the sanctum of your familiar incarceration..." Sounds artificial.
- **Too easy**: "Always believe in yourself." No new insight.
- **Better**: "Choose the sky before the cage begins to look like home."

### The caption
One or two unadorned, precise sentences describing the life lesson, its implications and a real situation where it applies. **Caption is not a second parable.** Exact text should not drift between review and publishing. Example:
**Image:** "Never ask the man selling umbrellas whether a storm is coming."
**Caption:** "People often give advice that serves their interests. Understand incentives before trusting recommendations."

### Test before images
Provide an insight in ordinary prose; then craft line; then specify a scene. If the drawing is the first inspiration, reverse-check that the takeaway is actually worth learning before proceeding. Do not build empty messages merely to show a charming creature.
